# Composition

Guidelines for play areas such as skate plazas, race courses, arenas, and town blocks. Adjust to the genre.

## Scale

- Pick the player (character plus vehicle) as the unit. Measure ramps, rails, stairs, curbs, doors, benches, and buildings against it, and write the key dimensions down.
- Use one scale for everything. Mixed-scale kits are the most common reason a space looks fake; rescale imports to the shared unit.
- Real reference sizes help: curb about 15 cm, bench seat about 45 cm, handrail about 90 cm, door about 2.1 m, one building storey about 3 m, lane about 3–3.5 m.

## Density and space

- Prefer compact over large. Empty ground between features reads as a prototype. Size the area so the next feature is visible from the last one.
- Group features into lines the player can chain (approach, feature, landing, next approach).
- Keep the main lines of travel clear of props, trees, and poles; dress the edges instead.
- Leave run-up and landing space proportional to the speed needed.

## Readability

- Landmarks the player can navigate by: a tall structure, a distinct color, a sign.
- Surface materials that tell the player what is rideable or walkable.
- Lighting that gives depth: a lower sun angle for longer shadows, ambient occlusion where the budget allows, and contrast between the play area and its surroundings.

## Context

- Surround the play area with believable edges: sidewalks, roads, fences, buildings, trees, signage, parked vehicles. The space should feel like part of a larger world, not an island.
- Backdrop terrain (hills, skyline) in the same art direction as the play area; low-poly hills next to realistic textures look out of place. Smooth, layered silhouettes usually work better than spiky ones.
- Distant context can be cheap: billboards, low-detail meshes, fog.

## Process

1. Sketch the layout top-down with dimensions (ASCII or a quick diagram) before placing anything.
2. Place features as data, not by hand-tuned coordinates scattered through rendering code.
3. Ride the lines in a scenario test and in the game; adjust spacing from what you see.
4. Screenshot from the player's eye height and from above, before and after.
